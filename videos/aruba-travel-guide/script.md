## Welcome to Aruba

Aruba is just nineteen point six miles long. It sits outside the hurricane belt, and every stretch of sand is public. Let's plan it properly, from the landing to the quiet coves.

So, where are we? Aruba lies in the southern Caribbean Sea, about fifteen to eighteen miles north of Venezuela's Paraguaná peninsula. It's six miles across, home to around a hundred and ten thousand people, and the climate is tropical semi-arid: warm and sunny all year, with only the occasional brief shower. The capital is Oranjestad.
<!-- f80029 f80025 f80031 f80033 f80035 -->

Dutch and Papiamento are the official languages, and most Arubans also speak English and Spanish, so you'll manage fine. Still, a cheerful bon dia in the morning goes a long way. The currency is the Aruban florin, US dollars are accepted in most places, and the island keeps Atlantic Standard Time all year, with no daylight saving.

## Before you fly

The paperwork is light, but it's checked before you board, not after you land. Every traveller to Aruba, including infants and children, must complete the online ED-Card, the Embarkation and Disembarkation Card, and get it approved. You can fill it in within seven days before your trip, and the airline will ask for proof of approval before you fly. Even passengers in transit need one.
<!-- f80267 f80051 f80268 f80244 f80246 -->

You'll need a valid passport. American, Canadian, Caribbean, European and most South American citizens don't need a visa, and British citizens can visit for up to three months without one. Everyone else should check the visa rules before booking.
<!-- f80050 f80049 f80242 -->

Two more items for the list. Aruba charges a sustainability fee to visitors arriving by air, and the tourism authority keeps its traveller requirements page up to date, so check the current details there rather than trusting an old blog post. And sort out travel health insurance; the UK government notes that European and Global Health Insurance Cards aren't valid in Aruba.
<!-- f80269 f80066 f80257 f80258 -->

Pack mineral-based sunscreen, the kind with zinc oxide or titanium dioxide as the active ingredient, and a cloth bag, because plastic bags are banned. The tourism authority even invites you to drink the water straight from the tap, so bring a refillable bottle.
<!-- f80084 f80085 f80089 f80086 -->

## From the airport to your hotel

Most visitors fly into Queen Beatrix International Airport, which has frequent nonstop or one-stop flights from most major US cities, and daily flights from major hubs around the world. Everyone clears immigration on arrival, and eligible travellers can use e-gates to move faster. Anything that may be prohibited or taxable has to be declared at customs.
<!-- f80048 f80260 f80262 f80245 -->

Now, the ride to your hotel. Most resorts sit on the northwestern coast, with the high-rises at Palm Beach and the low-rise resorts around quieter Eagle Beach. Taxis are one option, and the UK government's advice is simple: agree the fare with the driver, in local currency, before you set off.
<!-- f80069 f80070 f80054 f80254 -->

The public bus is a good way to explore later, but it's not built for arrival day: Arubus doesn't take luggage, only carry-on items. You can rent a car, motorcycle or scooter at the airport, though rental companies often ask for more than you'd expect, such as two years of driving experience, a higher minimum age or an international driving permit.

## When to go

Aruba's weather is steady enough that the calendar matters more than the forecast. Temperatures average between eighty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, about twenty-seven to thirty-two Celsius, and rain is rare. The year has three seasons: rainy from October to December, dry from January to April, and windy from May to October. Even in the rainy season, a whole day of rain is unusual.
<!-- f80200 f80203 f80202 -->

Peak season runs from December to April, when the island gets busy and accommodation and activities tend to cost more. The off-season, from September to November, brings substantial savings on rooms, flights, rental cars and activities, plus smaller crowds. The trade-off is a higher chance of sporadic rain, a higher heat index, and some restaurants closing for renovations or holidays.
<!-- f80208 f80216 f80207 f80213 f80212 f80214 -->

And hurricanes? Aruba lies well south of the main Atlantic hurricane belt, and hurricanes there are extremely rare. The regional season still runs from June to November, and the island can feel side effects from storms passing through the region, so a flexible booking makes sense in those months.
<!-- f80205 f80209 f80206 -->

If you like a party, Carnival in January and February is a popular time to visit; it's a months-long celebration and the island's biggest party of the year. If you like a breeze, the windy season brings Hi-Winds, the annual kiteboarding and windsurfing competition, in May.
<!-- f80210 f80281 f80204 -->

## The beaches

All of Aruba's beaches are open to everyone, and there are more than twenty public beaches and coves. The rule of thumb is simple: the west and south coasts are calm and protected, good for swimming and snorkelling, while the east and north coasts face the open sea, with stronger winds, crashing waves and big views.
<!-- f80117 f80052 f80114 f80115 -->

Eagle Beach is the long stretch of soft white sand with the famous fofoti trees, which visitors are asked to treat with respect. Up at the northern tip, on the road to the California Lighthouse, Arashi Beach has parking, a beach bar, shaded huts and chair rentals, and the best snorkelling is on the left as you face the sea. Nearby Boca Catalina is a small bay with gentle waves and colourful reef fish just offshore.
<!-- f80118 f80082 f80179 f80180 f80181 f80119 -->

On the south coast, Mangel Halto is a secluded beach with calm, shallow water, mangroves and plenty of sea life. Near San Nicolás, Baby Beach is a half-moon of sand on a calm lagoon, the most child-friendly beach on the island, about a forty-five minute drive from the Palm Beach hotels. Where the bay opens to the ocean, snorkellers are asked to stay inside the marked area.
<!-- f80182 f80174 f80175 f80123 f80178 f80176 -->

On the east side, enjoy the scenery and save the swim. Boca Grandi suits kitesurfing and windsurfing, but its strong current makes it a poor place to swim. Wherever you are, don't feed the wildlife, never stand on the reef, and leave shells and coral where you found them. And if you want a spot under the free public palapas, arrive early.
<!-- f80126 f80121 f80128 f80067 f80127 -->

## Beyond the beach

Nearly twenty percent of Aruba is national park. Arikok National Park, in the heart of the island, protects desert sand, towering cacti, caves and original Indian rock drawings. You'll need a ticket to enter, and most tours are guided by park rangers. You can explore on foot, on horseback or by vehicle, and if you go off-road, drive carefully so you don't damage the landscape or harm protected species.
<!-- f80163 f80028 f80165 f80167 f80166 f80102 f80088 -->

The Ayo and Casibari rock formations are open to climb and explore. The Ayo area was once considered a sacred site by the island's original inhabitants, and its rock drawings date back thousands of years. Then there's Hooiberg, Dutch for haystack: a cone-shaped hill rising five hundred and forty feet, with about six hundred steps to the top. Bring water, and patience.
<!-- f80171 f80172 f80173 f80168 f80169 f80170 -->

In Oranjestad, Fort Zoutman is the oldest remaining building in the capital, completed in seventeen ninety-eight. Beside it stands the Willem the Third Tower, built in eighteen sixty-eight as both a lighthouse and a public clock. The downtown sits steps from the cruise port, with shops, restaurants and pastel architecture.
<!-- f80189 f80190 f80191 f80090 -->

In the south, San Nicolás began its transformation into Aruba's Art District in twenty sixteen, and its streets carry murals by local and international artists; mural tours start at the ArtisA gallery. And in Savaneta, Aruba's first capital, Zeerover is a fishermen's wharf where the fresh catch is cleaned and cooked in front of you.
<!-- f80197 f80198 f80199 f80194 f80193 -->

## Getting around and staying well

For day trips, a car makes the smaller coves easier to reach. Taxis and pre-booked private transfers are also options if you'd rather not drive. The bus can help along the hotel strip — L10A, L10B and L10C stop at Eagle, Palm and Arashi, with the central station in Oranjestad — but it does not run all night, so check the last return before a late dinner.
<!-- f80236 f80229 f80228 f80231 -->

A car makes the smaller coves easier to reach. Two rules: driving on the beach is forbidden, because the beach is a fragile ecosystem, and drink-driving is a serious offence. In and around Oranjestad, parking is paid with a rechargeable card.
<!-- f80125 f80087 f80253 f80063 -->

On safety, Canada advises normal security precautions for Aruba. Petty theft does happen, so leave valuables and cash in your room rather than taking them to the beach. Healthcare is very good, though facilities may ask for immediate payment, one more reason for that insurance. For emergencies, dial nine one one for an ambulance or fire, and one hundred for the police. And carry ID; it's a legal requirement.

## Your Aruba toolkit

So, the short version. Get your ED-Card approved before you fly. Pick your season on purpose. Check the official pages again just before you travel, because rules and fees can change. Pre-book the ride from the airport if you want an easy first hour. Swim on the calm west and south coasts, take the bus for easy daytime trips and a car for the coves, and keep your ID with you. Book the one activity you care about most first, and leave a little room in the plan. The rest is sunshine and good decisions about lunch.
